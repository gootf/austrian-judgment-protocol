# Patterns — Austrian Judgment Protocol

*Each pattern: When to use / How / Trade-offs. Provenance (Ch, page). Source: [F&K] = Foss & Klein; [Packard] = Packard.*

## Pattern 1: Opportunity Conjunction Check [Packard]
**When to use**: evaluating any potential venture or business opportunity before committing resources.
**How**: verify all three conditions: (1) a recognized, imperfectly satisfied consumer need (not assumed demand); (2) both needs knowledge and technical/affordance knowledge present in the solution effort; (3) a capitalist judgment of total value vs. total cost (including opportunity cost) and willingness to bear risk.
**Trade-offs**: rigorous but slow; can reject ideas that would succeed through iterative discovery of need (the process allows feedback loops between stages 1 and 2).
**Provenance**: source-derived (Ch2 p27-28; Ch5 p122-123).

## Pattern 2: Einstellung Audit (Expert Trap Detection) [Packard]
**When to use**: a technically strong team or expert proposes an innovation; when an organization's innovation output stalls despite deep expertise.
**How**: check knowledge balance — high technical/affordance knowledge without corresponding needs knowledge predicts entrenchment (innovativeness decreases, not increases, for one-sided experts). Acquire needs knowledge deliberately (user research, lead-user engagement) before solution commitment.
**Trade-offs**: needs knowledge is tacit and hard to transfer; lead-user engagement and open innovation (Chesbrough 2003) are partial fixes.
**Provenance**: source-derived (Ch3 p45, p123; Ch5 p123).

## Pattern 3: Three Judgments of the Capitalist [Packard]
**When to use**: before investing in or launching a venture — the resource-owner's decision point.
**How**: make three explicit estimations: (1) total expected cost and viability (incl. opportunity costs, wealth-buffer risk tolerance); (2) total economic value to consumers (demand + relative benefit) — corrected for the systematic bias that entrepreneurs overestimate how widely their own need is shared; (3) perception of the actor's ability to execute.
**Trade-offs**: the second judgment is systematically overconfident (Camerer & Lovallo 1999; De Meza & Southey 1996) — build in independent demand checks.
**Provenance**: source-derived (Ch2 p35-36).

## Pattern 4: Knowledge-Intersection Innovation [Packard]
**When to use**: designing innovation capability; diagnosing why innovation does or does not occur.
**How**: innovation = needs knowledge × technical knowledge (affordance knowledge). Both must be present; order of antecedents is flexible (affordance-first is more common; need-first requires uncertain search). Scientific discovery creates affordance spikes that generate waves of innovation (Nemet 2009).
**Trade-offs**: one-sided knowledge actively inhibits (Einstellung); accessibility (recent recall) facilitates combination — recency effects matter.
**Provenance**: source-derived (Ch3 p45, p49-59; Ch2 p29-32).

## Pattern 5: Consumer as Performance Arbiter [Packard]
**When to use**: forecasting adoption/failure of a new product; explaining diffusion outcomes.
**How**: model outcomes as consumer uncertainty resolution, not product-quality competition: uncertainty explains S-curve adoption, new-product failure, the chasm (Moore 1991), first-mover advantage sustainability, and continuous innovation extending product life beyond saturation.
**Trade-offs**: simulation-based (agent-based model) rather than field-validated; assumes individual-level decision criteria.
**Provenance**: source-derived (Ch4 p77-121; Ch5 p123).

## Pattern 6: Consumer-Led Opportunity Discovery [Packard]
**When to use**: sourcing new venture ideas; deciding who is most likely to innovate in a domain.
**How**: opportunities originate in consumers' recognition of their own unmet needs (learned through experience); the most valuable ideas come from lead users / high consumer involvement, not from producers' imagination of demand. Needs are discovered; solutions are created.
**Trade-offs**: needs are tacit — communication with consumers is "highly fallible"; producer-side needs knowledge is systematically incomplete.
**Provenance**: source-derived (Ch2 p13-14, p27; Ch3 p50).

## Pattern 7: Delegation Decision (Constrained Empowerment) [F&K]
**When to use**: deciding whether/how to delegate a decision right to an employee, team, or sub-agent.
**How**: (1) classify original vs derived; (2) identify the knowledge holder; (3) delegate with constraints from the plan; (4) monitor use; (5) reserve ultimate decision rights at top management.
**Trade-offs**: over-constraint kills productive proxy entrepreneurship; under-constraint invites destructive forms.
**Provenance**: source-derived (Original and derived judgment p192-196; Conclusions p219).

## Pattern 8: Contract Design for Entrepreneurship [F&K]
**When to use**: designing employment contracts, incentive systems, or agent agreements where judgment matters.
**How**: do NOT aim for complete contracts — completeness curbs entrepreneurial activity. Use organizational structure (rewards/punishments, dispute rules, evaluation) to balance productive vs destructive proxy entrepreneurship.
**Trade-offs**: informal systems are harder to change than contracts; the balance is context-specific.
**Provenance**: source-derived (Conclusions p219-220).

## Pattern 9: Authority-Knowledge Matching [F&K]
**When to use**: allocating decision rights in an organization or agent architecture.
**How**: authority should be backed by ownership and exercised by those who can judge capacity (not necessarily problem details); decision rights without monitoring or with mismatched knowledge produce malformed delegation.
**Trade-offs**: retaining too much authority centralizes noise; delegating without ownership basis dilutes accountability.
**Provenance**: source-derived (Original and derived judgment p193-194; Dispersed knowledge p213) + agent-derived application.

## Pattern 10: Information Filter Design [F&K]
**When to use**: designing reporting structures, or agent hierarchies that must cope with dispersed knowledge.
**How**: preserve middle-manager filters: they compress information upward and signal project legitimacy downward (resource allocation as signal).
**Trade-offs**: removing filters = centralization of noise; too many layers = signal dilution.
**Provenance**: source-derived (Dispersed knowledge/authority p213) + agent-derived application.
